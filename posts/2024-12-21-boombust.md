## The Myth of the "Natural" Business Cycle

The Austrian School of economics offers a radical and uncomfortable truth: **boom-bust cycles are not natural features of capitalism. They are artificial creations of government intervention — specifically, the manipulation of interest rates and credit markets by central banks.**

Unlike Keynesian economists, who argue that government intervention is necessary to stabilize the economy, Austrian economists contend that **it is government intervention itself that creates the instability.**

## The Mechanism: How Government Creates Booms — and Busts

The Austrian business cycle theory, pioneered by Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek, explains the boom-bust process as follows:

**Step 1: Artificial Credit Expansion**

Central banks lower interest rates below their natural market level. This is not the result of increased savings — it is the result of monetary manipulation. The artificially low interest rate sends a false signal to the economy: it suggests that society has more savings available for investment than it actually does.

**Step 2: Malinvestment**

Entrepreneurs, responding to these false signals, embark on projects that would not be profitable under natural interest rates. Capital is misallocated — diverted into unsustainable ventures, speculative real estate, and asset bubbles. This is **malinvestment**: investment in projects that appear profitable only because of artificially cheap credit.

**Step 3: The Artificial Boom**

The economy appears to thrive. Employment rises, asset prices surge, and confidence is high. But this boom is hollow — it is built on credit, not savings. The structure of production is distorted, with resources allocated to projects that cannot survive when reality catches up.

**Step 4: The Bust**

Eventually, the truth cannot be ignored. Resources are scarce. The malinvestments become evident. The boom collapses into bust — and with it comes recession, unemployment, and widespread financial ruin.

## The Hidden Tax: Who Pays the Price?

Here is the most important — and most overlooked — aspect of the boom-bust cycle: **the costs are never shared equally.**

### Those Who Profit from the Boom

| Beneficiary | How They Gain |
|-------------|--------------|
| **Financial institutions** | Expand credit at near-zero cost, earn spreads on risky loans |
| **Large corporations** | Access cheap capital for speculation and buybacks |
| **Asset holders** | Stock and real estate prices surge |
| **Politicians** | Short-term employment growth boosts approval ratings |

### Those Who Pay the Price

| Victim | How They Suffer |
|--------|----------------|
| **Retail investors** | Enter at peak prices, lose everything when the bubble bursts |
| **Individual borrowers** | Face margin calls, mortgage defaults, and bankruptcy |
| **Fixed-income earners** | See savings eroded by inflation during the boom, unemployment during the bust |
| **Ordinary workers** | Lose jobs when malinvestments collapse |
| **Pensioners and savers** | See purchasing power destroyed by inflation and low interest rates |

### The Ultimate Injustice: Privatized Gains, Socialized Losses

When the bust comes, the pattern repeats:

- **Financial institutions** that bet recklessly during the boom receive government bailouts — taxpayer money to cover losses they created.
- **Retail investors** receive nothing. They are told to "stay the course" while their life savings evaporate.
- **Politicians** who enabled the boom face no consequences. They simply prepare for the next cycle.

This is what Austrian economists call **moral hazard**: when the consequences of risk-taking are privatized for the few and socialized for the many.

## Historical Evidence: The Federal Reserve Era

Consider the historical record:

**Before the Federal Reserve (pre-1913):**

- Business cycles existed, but recoveries were typically **short and shallow**.
- Without a central bank to inject artificial credit, booms could not become as inflated, and busts could not become as devastating.
- Bank failures occurred, but they were contained — not bailed out, not normalized.

**After the Federal Reserve (post-1913):**

- **Boom-bust cycles became larger, more frequent, and more devastating.**
- 1920s boom → 1929 Great Depression (fuelled by Fed's easy money in the 1920s)
- 1990s dot-com bubble → 2000 crash (Fed's easy money)
- 2000s housing bubble → 2008 Financial Crisis (Fed's ultra-low rates)
- 2020 pandemic easing → 2021–2024 inflation and asset bubbles

The pattern is unmistakable: **every major economic crisis since the Fed's creation was preceded by artificial credit expansion.**

## The Austrian Prescription

What should be done?

**1. End central bank manipulation of interest rates.** Let market savings determine interest rates naturally.

**2. Stop government industrial policy and subsidies.** Politicians do not know which industries will succeed — only markets do.

**3. Allow malinvestments to fail.** Painful as it is, allowing bad investments to collapse is the only way to restore capital to productive uses.

**4. Abolish or fundamentally reform the Federal Reserve.** The Austrian School has long argued that central banking is inherently destabilizing.

## Conclusion: The Austrian Warning

The Austrian School's warning is clear and urgent: **every government intervention aimed at "managing" the economy creates the conditions for the next crisis.** The boom is a lie. The bust is the bill coming due. And the people who pay it — always the same people — are the ones least responsible for creating the problem.

Understanding the Austrian perspective is not about choosing investment strategies. It is about recognizing a fundamental truth: **when governments manipulate markets, the costs are never borne equally.** And in a free market without intervention, booms do not become bubbles — and busts do not become depressions.
